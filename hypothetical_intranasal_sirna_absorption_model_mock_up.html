Hypothetical Intranasal siRNA Absorption Model Mock Up Version 02

  • Objective. Decide whether an HTR2A siRNA gets into brain tissue better as a 5 nm lipid conjugate or a 180 nm yeast extracellular vesicle, and which of two nasal devices to pair with it. Endpoint is siRNA entering brain tissue, as a share of the dose sprayed into the nose. Distribution within the brain is out of scope.
  • Carrier and device are one decision. The conjugate does best on the Rocket Device, 0.036% of the sprayed dose. The vesicle does best on the MetP Device, 0.024%. The vesicle on the Rocket Device is the worst of the four arms, 0.014% — it pays full mucus cost to reach a nerve route it cannot use.
  • The epithelium is the barrier, not the mucus. Both carriers lose over 95% of what reaches the nasal lining at the moment of crossing it. Neither device changes that. Mucus favours the conjugate, the epithelium favours the vesicle, and the two roughly cancel.
  • No yEV figure here was measured on a yeast vesicle. Uptake rates come from mammalian exosomes 9, nerve-route penalties from rigid polymer particles 11. Yeast vesicles lack the mammalian surface proteins that produced those rates and carry mannoproteins and β-glucan that could route them into immune cells instead 15.
  • Two bench experiments would settle it, neither needing an animal. Track labelled yEV in fresh nasal mucus to fix the mucus penalty, which spans 5× to 40,000× in the literature 1. Then run both carriers at matched siRNA dose across an air-liquid interface olfactory epithelium, with a readout that separates neuronal uptake from uptake by resident immune cells.

Why the answer has changed three times
Each revision corrected a real error. Recording them here so the current number is read with the right amount of confidence, which is not much.
Answer given What was wrong The correction
280× conjugate The vesicle was charged for its size four separate times. Stokes–Einstein, a Ferry–Renkin steric pore term, a hand-set mucoadhesion factor, and the same pore mathematics again at the nerve step. The pore term also contradicted its own source: measured coated 200 nm particles move 4–6× slower than water 1, where the pore model predicted roughly 1,500×. Intact polymeric nanoparticles, PEGylated or not, do not enter the olfactory bulb from the nasal cavity, though they do permeate trigeminal nerves 11. A 10× penalty now applies on the olfactory route and near-parity on the trigeminal.

Parameters
Every input, with its citation. Numbers marked none have no source and were set by this model. Numbers marked other system are real measurements made in a different organism or on a different material.

Carrier

Radius sets free diffusion speed. Mucus slowing is a separate measured penalty, so size is not counted twice.

Nasal mucus

How many times slower each carrier moves in mucus than in water. Published tracking spans 5× for a stealth-coated particle to 40,000× for a mucoadhesive one.

Epithelial crossing

Intact tight junctions exclude both carriers. Raise the junction radius to simulate an absorption enhancer. Uptake is per particle, per residence period.

Routes to brain

Material crossing the epithelium takes a nerve route or perivascular bulk flow. Vesicle penalties differ by route because the two nerves treat a large particle very differently.

Rocket Device

Cleft-targeted narrow plume. Raises the share of dose landing in the olfactory cleft.

MetP Device

Viscous controlled-release vehicle, not a targeting device. Acts by extending residence.

Dose and sampling

Half-life converts siRNA delivered into siRNA still detectable when the animal is sampled. Arms sampled at different times cannot be compared without it.

Why a yeast vesicle is not a mammalian one
Every uptake number above was measured on mammalian extracellular vesicles. None was measured on a yeast vesicle. The program's own specifications set out why that substitution is unsafe.
Property Mammalian EV yEV Effect on uptake
SterolCholesterol, 30–35 mol%Ergosterol, 25–30 mol% Ergosterol stiffens the membrane. A less deformable vesicle behaves more like the rigid polymer particles that failed to enter the olfactory bulb 11, which argues the 10× nerve penalty is not overstated.
Surface proteinsCD9, CD63, CD81, integrinsNone of these The 1% uptake rate was competed away by excess unlabelled vesicles and varied by cell type 10. It measured specific recognition. A yeast vesicle presents no such ligands, so the rate has no basis for carrying over.
GlycosylationMammalian glycansMannoproteins, Man8–Man15 Mannose receptor CD206 could drive uptake the mammalian work never tested. Program mouse data found mannose receptor blockade reduced yEV brain uptake 15, so the route is real.
β-glucanAbsentPresent unless purified out A Dectin-1 ligand. With CD206, this raises a possibility the model does not represent: lamina propria macrophages and dendritic cells taking up yEV faster than neurons do. In a tissue homogenate that reads as absorption while being a dead end.

The direction of this error is unknown. Losing tetraspanins and integrins argues the true yEV uptake rate is below 1%. Gaining mannose and glucan receptor engagement argues above it, but possibly into the wrong cells. Both are testable in one air-liquid interface experiment, provided the readout distinguishes neurons from resident immune cells rather than measuring bulk tissue signal.

Does cargo capacity let the vesicle catch up?
A vesicle carries many siRNA at once and a conjugate carries one. The question is whether raising the vesicle's payload can close the gap. The answer depends entirely on what limits the dose, and there are two regimes.

Regime one: dose limited by siRNA. Capacity does nothing at all. Give both arms the same number of siRNA molecules. The vesicle arm then contains N/L vesicles carrying L each. Every barrier in this model acts on the particle, so the fraction arriving is the same whatever L is, and each surviving vesicle still delivers L. The L cancels exactly: delivered siRNA is N × P either way. Under a matched-dose comparison there is no break-even capacity, because capacity is not in the equation.

This is worth stating because it is easy to assume the opposite. Loading more siRNA per vesicle does not make the vesicle a better carrier. It makes each successful crossing worth more and successful crossings proportionally rarer.

Regime two: dose limited by volume. Capacity is the whole argument. A nose takes about 200 µL. Within that volume the conjugate is limited by how concentrated a solution can be made, and the vesicle by how many particles a suspension can hold. Those ceilings are wildly different, and now L determines how much siRNA the vesicle arm can physically contain. A break-even capacity exists, and it can be calculated.
